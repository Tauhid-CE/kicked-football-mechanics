Abstract

A kicked football rarely follows the textbook parabola: it bends sideways, and longer kicks fly higher. This paper starts from Newton’s second law, builds the differential equations of the ball in flight (gravity, air drag, Magnus force, optional spin decay), and shows why they have no closed-form solution. They are then solved numerically with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method. The solver is tested against the exact vacuum solution and against step-size convergence. It is then used to compare a short pass with a long kick and to show how height and sideways curve grow with distance. The foot only sets the launch conditions; after contact, the ball flies freely. It also shows that the swing of the foot before contact is irrelevant (the ball has no memory), maps where on the ball’s surface to strike for a chosen amount of curve, and finds that in this model the curve stops growing once the contact offset reaches about one third of the ball’s radius. The model assumes calm air, a rigid ball and flat ground (Section 2). The paper ends with a field guide to where on the ball to strike so that both a short pass and a long kick bend, without extra kicking power. A long kick needs more upward launch and gets its curve from the distance itself, so it is struck low and close to the centre line; a short pass is struck level with the middle and well out to the side.

1The Problem

A basic physics book models a kicked ball like a rock released from a rope: it leaves along a tangent and then only gravity acts. The path is a parabola inside one vertical plane, with no sideways drift.

In reality the ball drifts sideways, so something else is acting.

The foot does not steer the ball in the air. One might think the curve comes from the foot: the leg bends, the foot swings along a curved path, and the ball “follows” it. It does not. The ball has no memory of the foot’s path. Contact lasts about 0.01 s, and in that instant the foot delivers an impulse at one point of the ball’s surface. That impulse sets only the launch conditions:

  • the launch velocity v0​ (speed and direction),
  • the launch spin ω0​ (axis and rate).

After separation the ball is free. The curve itself comes from the physics of the flight. So the real unknowns are v0​ and ω0​.

The swing before contact does not matter (the ball has no memory). Many people believe that how the foot moves before contact (a wide curved swing, a straight one, a long run-up) shapes the flight. It does not, except through the contact it produces. The equations of motion in Section 6.4 are first-order in the state q = (p, v, ω): the future of the ball is fixed completely by its state at the instant of separation. Nothing about the earlier path of the foot is stored in the ball. Two kicks with very different swings, but the same contact point, the same force direction and the same impulse, produce the same v0​ and ω0​ and therefore exactly the same flight. The swing is only a means of delivering a chosen contact reliably, and that is the only sense in which it matters to a player.

The assumptions behind the model (calm air, a rigid ball, flat ground and so on) are collected in Section 2.

2Assumptions

Every result in this paper holds only under the following assumptions. They are stated first so that it is clear what the model leaves out.

Still air
There is no wind: the air is at rest relative to the ground, and the ball moves through it. If the air moves, the drag and Magnus forces depend on the velocity of the ball relative to the air, v − vair. A crosswind then pushes the ball sideways on top of the spin curve (it can add to it or cancel it), a headwind increases drag and shortens the kick, and a tailwind does the opposite. These effects are not computed here; on the pitch the player simply re-aims.
Uniform air
The air density is constant along the flight (ρ = 1.1515 kg/m³, Section 4). Peak heights are below about 8 m, so the change of density with height is negligible.
Rigid ball
A solid sphere of mass 0.43 kg and radius 0.11 m (size 5). Its deformation matters only during contact and is hidden inside the factor η of Section 3.
Instant contact
The foot touches the ball for about 0.01 s and sets only the launch velocity and the launch spin. After separation the ball is free and has no memory of the foot.
Side spin
The spin axis is vertical (pure side spin) and the spin rate is constant. Spin decay is an option (Section 6.4 and Section 9.4); tilted axes are discussed in Section 11.
Fixed coefficients
Drag coefficient CD = 0.25, and a lift coefficient that grows with the spin parameter and saturates at 0.25 (Section 5). Seam effects and knuckle-ball motion are ignored.
Flat ground
Level, firm ground. The ball leaves at ground level, lands at the same height and nothing is in the way.

These assumptions make the model clean enough to solve and to reason about. The field guide in Section 10 uses only the shape of the results, not their exact values, so it is not sensitive to the small errors they introduce.

3How the Kick Creates v0​ and ω0​

Let r be the contact point measured from the ball’s centre (∣r∣=R) and J the total impulse from the foot. The ball starts at rest, so:

mv0​Iω0​I​=J=r×J=κI​mR2​​(linear impulse gives velocity)(torque impulse gives spin)(κI​=32​ thin shell, 52​ solid sphere)​(1)

Combining, for a shell:

ω0​=η2R23(r×v0​)​,∣ω0​∣=η2R23v0​d​(2)

where d is the perpendicular distance from the centre to the line of the impulse. The factor η (between 0 and 1) is a calibration constant for friction and ball deformation.

Two honest remarks. First, if the force line passes through the centre, r×J=0 and there is no spin and no Magnus curve. Second, a force acting normal to a sphere’s surface always passes through its centre, so real spin comes from the tangential (friction) part of the contact force and from deformation. This is why η must be learned from measurement or practice rather than computed.

The spin parameter at launch is S0​=v0​R∣ω0​∣​=2R3ηd​. It depends only on the contact offset d, not on the kick speed. So the same technique (same offset) gives the same S0​ at any distance. This is used in Section 9.

3.1 Where on the ball to strike (the contact map)

The contact offset d fixes the spin, so the point of the ball’s surface that the foot meets fixes the curve. Let φ be the angle at the centre between the line of the force and the contact point (the contact point sits at angle φ from the centre line, with d=Rsinφ). Then

S0​=2R3ηd​=23η​sinφ(3)

Three rules follow.

  1. Through the centre (d = 0, φ = 0): no spin, no Magnus force, no curve.
  2. Level with the ball’s middle, to one side: the horizontal offset gives spin about a vertical axis (side spin), and the Magnus force is horizontal. This is the pure bend. A contact point below the middle gives backspin (the Magnus force lifts the ball: higher, longer hang time); above the middle gives topspin (the ball dips). A mix of side and vertical offset tilts the spin axis and gives bend plus lift or dip.
  3. Diminishing returns: with the law CL​(S)=min(0.25,0.5S) of Section 5, the lift coefficient saturates at S = 0.5. The curve therefore stops growing once S0​ reaches about 0.5, which corresponds to d∗=3ηR​ and φ∗=arcsin3η1​ ≈ 19.5° for η = 1 (d∗ ≈ 3.7 cm on this ball). Beyond d∗ a larger offset adds no curve in this model. In real play a glancing blow also transfers less forward speed to the ball (not modelled here), so d∗ is the largest sensible offset.

Because S0​ depends only on d and not on v0​, the same offset gives the same S0​ at any distance. What changes with distance is how much that spin matters, because the sideways deflection grows roughly as L2 (Section 9.3). Section 9.6 quantifies this.

6Deriving the Differential Equations

6.1 Coordinates

x = downrange (the kick direction), y = sideways (left is positive), z = up. Position p = (x, y, z), velocity v = dp/dt, spin vector ω. The ball’s centre starts at p0​ = (x0​, y0​, z0​), with z0​ = R on the ground.

6.2 Newton's second law

mdt2d2p​=Fgravity​+Fdrag​+FMagnus​(6)

6.3 The three forces

Gravity:

Fg​=−mgz^(7)

Drag opposes the velocity and grows with speed squared:

FD​=−21​ρCD​A∣v∣v(8)

Magnus force: perpendicular to both the spin axis and the velocity. Its size is (1/2) ρ A CL​(S) |v|² times the sine of the angle ψ between spin axis and velocity, so it vanishes when the spin axis lies along the velocity:

FM​=21​ρACL​(S)∣v∣(ω^×v),ω^=∣ω∣ω​(9)

Direction check: a ball moving in +x with side spin ω = +ωz ẑ gets z^×x^=y^​, so it curves left. Backspin (ω along −ŷ) gives (−y^​)×x^=+z^, a lift force upward. Both agree with experience.

Divide by m and define two constants:

kD​kL​​=2mρCD​A​=2mρA​​​(drag, unit m−1)(lift, unit m−1)​(10)

The ball’s state is nine numbers: position (3), velocity (3), spin (3). The system is:

and the spin equation:

dtdω​=−λω(λ=0 gives constant spin; exact solution ω=ω0​e−λt)(13)

with initial conditions p(0) = p0​, v(0) = v0​, ω(0) = ω0​ from Section 3. For example, with ρ = 1.1515: kD​=0.01272 m−1 and kL​=0.0509 m−1.

6.5 Why there is no closed-form solution

Look at the drag term −kD​ |v| v. The factor |v| = √(vx² + vy² + vz²) mixes all three components, so the three velocity equations cannot be solved one by one. The Magnus term adds more coupling: its direction rotates as v changes, and its size depends on S = R|ω|/|v|, which depends on |v| as well. The result is a coupled nonlinear system, and in general x(t), y(t), z(t) have no formula in terms of elementary functions.

Exact solutions exist only in special cases:

  • no drag and no spin: the parabola (Section 6.6);
  • linear drag (−k v) with constant spin: also solvable, but that replaces the real quadratic drag by a simplification.

For the real model, the honest route is numerical integration (Section 7).

6.6 The one exact case: vacuum (the check for the solver)

With kD​ = 0 and no spin, the equations integrate directly:

x(t)y(t)z(t)​=x0​+v0​cosθt=y0​=z0​+v0​sinθt−21​gt2​(14)

giving, for landing at launch height:

flight timeTrangeLpeak heightH​=g2v0​sinθ​=gv02​sin2θ​=2gv02​sin2θ​=4L​tanθ​(15)

The last form shows why a longer kick is also higher: at a given launch angle, H is proportional to L (and players raise θ for long kicks, making it grow faster).

7Numerical Solution

7.1 The idea

Write the system compactly as dtdq​=f(q), with state

q=(x,y,z,vx​,vy​,vz​,ωx​,ωy​,ωz​)(16)

and f the right-hand sides of Section 6.4. Starting from q(0), step forward in time by a small step h.

7.2 Euler method (simple, but inaccurate)

q(t+h)≈q(t)+hf(q(t))(17)

Its error per unit time is proportional to h (first-order). It needs very small steps.

7.3 Fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4), the method used

k1​k2​k3​k4​q(t+h)​=f(q)=f(q+2h​k1​)=f(q+2h​k2​)=f(q+hk3​)=q(t)+6h​(k1​+2k2​+2k3​+k4​)​(18)

It samples the slope four times per step, and its error scales as h⁴. The step is h = 0.001 s in the results below.

7.4 Detecting the landing

Stop at the first step where the ball’s centre falls below the launch height z0​ (= R). Then linearly interpolate between the last step above and the first step below to estimate the landing time and position. This is more accurate than using the first below-ground step.

7.5 Algorithm in steps

  1. Compute ρ from pressure, temperature, humidity (Section 4).
  2. Choose v0​, elevation θ, and the spin vector ω0​ (from the contact offset, Section 3).
  3. Build q(0) and the constants kD​, kL​.
  4. Repeat RK4 steps until z < z0​.
  5. Interpolate the landing and record the table of (t, x, y, z). These sampled values are the position functions x(t), y(t), z(t).

The Python program football_ode_solver.py (delivered with this paper) implements all of this using only numpy.

9.4 Effect of spin decay

With λ = 0.03 per second the long-kick sideways deflection drops from 4.78 m to 4.67 m (about 2% less) and the landing point barely moves (41.83 m to 41.85 m). The decay rate was assumed, not measured, so this is an order-of-magnitude statement: for a typical flight constant spin is a good approximation, and real decay slightly reduces the late-flight curve. The same program handles any λ by changing one number.

These are outputs of a simplified model with assumed coefficients. They show the structure of the effects (height grows with distance, the curve grows faster than distance, spin changes the landing point). They are not measured data. The absolute size of the curve depends strongly on the assumed CL​ law and should be calibrated against filmed kicks.

10From Theory to the Field: Where to Kick

The earlier sections explain why the ball curves. A player on the pitch needs something different: a decision about where on the ball to put the foot. This section turns the results into that decision, for both a short pass and a long kick.

10.1 Why a rough guide is enough

A player cannot solve equations during a kick; the body learns from repeated kicks (a regression machine). So the guide below gives only a starting point in centimetres, and practice fine-tunes it.

10.2 What the contact point controls

Look at the ball from behind, along the kick direction (Figure 6). The place where the foot meets the ball has two independent directions, and each controls one thing.

Sideways offset
How far the contact is to the left or right of the centre line sets the side spin, and so the curve. Left and right are your own left and right, as you stand behind the ball looking at the target. To curve the ball to your left, strike the half of the ball on your right-hand side; to curve it to your right, strike the half on your left-hand side (the direction check of Section 6.3).
Height below centre
How far below the middle the foot meets the ball sets the launch elevation. When the foot meets the ball squarely, the ball leaves along the line through its centre, so for a contact a height e below the middle, sin θ ≈ e/R. A contact level with the middle gives a flat launch; one lower down gives a steeper one.

Distance fixes how much upward launch is needed. A long kick always needs an upward displacement: the peak height grows in proportion to the range, about one tenth of it (Section 9.3), so a kick of about 40 m must climb about 7 m. A short pass needs very little height, and a low pass is also easier to control and harder to intercept. This decides the vertical part of the contact point, while the sideways part is decided by how much bend is wanted.

Rule of thumbThe nearer the target, the more level and the further out to the side you strike. The farther the target, the lower and the closer to the centre line you strike. A long kick is struck low (for the height it needs) and almost centrally (the distance supplies the curve); a short pass is struck level with the middle and clearly to the side (it needs the full offset, and gets only a small bend).

Two honest limits. First, a short pass can bend only a little: even at the largest useful offset the 8.65 m pass moves about 0.48 m, because the curve builds up late in the flight (Section 9.6). A larger bend over a short distance cannot be had by striking harder or further out. Second, the numbers in the table assume the idealised contact of Section 3 with η = 1. A real foot slides and the ball deforms, so the player should treat them as a starting point and let practice set the exact offsets.

10.4 Why this does not need extra kicking energy

The curve is obtained by placing the foot, not by kicking harder. The spin parameter depends only on the contact offset and not on the kick speed, and for the same technique the sideways curve grows about as the square of the distance. A long kick therefore collects its large curve from its own flight time, at no cost in effort. It also needs the speed it already has: 27 m/s against 15 m/s for the short pass is 3.2 times the kinetic energy. This is the reason the long kick should keep a small offset. A large offset is a glancing blow, and in real play a glancing blow transfers less forward speed (this loss is not modelled here), which would hurt exactly where speed is needed.

The short pass is the opposite case. It is cheap, so it can afford the large offset, and the plateau at R/3 tells the player where to stop: beyond it there is nothing more to gain and some speed to lose.

The sensitivity differs as well. In this model one millimetre of offset moves the landing point sideways by about 0.4 m on the long kick, about 0.1 m on the medium pass and about 0.014 m on the short pass. The long kick is the most sensitive, which is why its offset is learned by feel and corrected by the aim, and the short pass is the most forgiving.

10.5 How to learn it

The swing of the foot is worth practising only because it delivers the same contact point and the same foot speed again and again; the ball receives nothing else from it. Practice should therefore be structured: change one input at a time (for example, move the contact point 1 cm outward at the same foot speed), kick several times at each of a few distances, record where the ball crosses a target line, and compare averages, not single shots. Theory gives the shape of the map and the starting point of Figure 6; practice fixes its numbers, including the personal factor η.

11Limits

  • CD​ and CL​ are assumed. They vary with speed, spin, seams and ball type; at some speeds drag changes sharply (drag crisis).
  • Contact is idealised through η. Real spin depends on friction and deformation.
  • Only constant or exponentially decaying spin is modelled; the spin axis is fixed in the ball’s launch frame (no wobble).
  • No wind. With wind, replace v by v − v_air in the drag and Magnus terms.
  • Seam effects and wake instabilities (knuckleball-type motion) are not included; a ball with almost no spin can still deviate.
  • The spin axis is modelled as vertical. A purely horizontal offset on a lofted kick (elevation θ) actually tilts the axis by about θ from the vertical, perpendicular to v0​. The Magnus force is then purely sideways and larger by roughly 1/cos θ at launch (about 15% at 30°).
  • The plateau at d∗ = R/(3η) comes from the assumed CL​ law. It is a structural result of the model, not a measurement; the real plateau must be found from tracked kicks.
  • The cost of a very large offset (less forward speed from a glancing blow) is not modelled.
  • Results are illustrative until compared with filmed, tracked kicks.
  • The strike points of Section 10 are rules of thumb from the idealised contact model. They assume the foot meets the ball squarely, so that the ball leaves along the line through its centre; a real foot slides, and the player must calibrate the numbers by practice.

12Conclusion

The curve of a kicked football is a flight phenomenon: the foot sets v0​ and ω0​ and then lets go. Newton’s law with gravity, quadratic drag and the Magnus force gives a nine-variable coupled nonlinear system with no closed-form solution, so it is solved with RK4. The solver reproduces the exact vacuum solution and converges quickly with step size. With the same technique, height grows about linearly with distance while the sideways curve grows about as the square of the distance. Neither the swing before contact nor anything else in the foot’s history matters, only the state at separation. For a pure bend the contact point is level with the middle of the ball and off to one side, and a lofted kick moves it lower; in this model the curve grows with the offset until d = R/3 (about 3.7 cm) and then stops, and short passes can bend only a little because the deflection builds up late in the flight. Theory explains the forces; deliberate practice teaches a player how to reproduce a chosen flight.

For use on the field, the results reduce to one rule that needs no calculation: the nearer the target, the more level and the further out to the side the foot meets the ball; the farther the target, the lower and the closer to the centre line. A player does not compute this; the body learns the numbers by practice, and the theory shows where to start and where extra effort stops helping.

  • Spin, not the swingThe foot only sets launch speed and spin. After contact the ball flies free.
  • Curve ∝ distance²With the same technique, height grows with range and the sideways curve about as its square.
  • Long kickStrike low (for the height) and almost on the centre line. The distance supplies the curve.
  • Short passStrike level with the middle and out to the side, up to R/3 (3.7 cm). Beyond that nothing is gained.
